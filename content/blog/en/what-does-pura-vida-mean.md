If you are learning Costa Rican Spanish, this is the first expression you will hear and the last one you will fully understand. Not because it is complicated, but because **it does not mean one single thing**.

## The five things it means

Same phrase, different context and tone:

| Situation | What it means |
|---|---|
| Someone greets you | "Hello" |
| Someone says goodbye | "See you" |
| You are asked how you are | "All good" |
| Someone helps you | "Thank you" |
| You apologise for something minor | "No worries" |

Notice that the last two are opposites: one is said by the person receiving the favour, the other by the person doing it. Tone resolves the ambiguity without anyone having to think about it.

## Where it comes from

The most commonly cited origin is a 1956 Mexican film titled *¡Pura vida!*, starring the comedian Antonio Espino, known as "Clavillazo", whose character repeated the phrase with stubborn optimism while everything went wrong for him. The film was shown in Costa Rica and the expression stuck.

It is worth being honest about this: it is the most repeated and most plausible explanation, but the journey of a line from a film into a country's daily speech does not leave a receipt. What is documented is that its widespread use in Costa Rica postdates those years.

## How to use it without sounding like a tourist

Three practical rules:

1. **Do not use it for everything.** The most common mistake newcomers make is answering "pura vida" to absolutely anything. It sounds like a memorised formula.
2. **Turn the volume down.** It is said casually, not as a celebration. A shouted "pura vida" gives away a visitor faster than an accent does.
3. **It is good for closing.** It works beautifully to end a short exchange: you pay for something, you get your change, you say "pura vida" and you leave. There it is perfect.

## What it really communicates

"Pura vida" is not just a greeting: it is a way of taking the weight off things. When someone tells you about a medium-sized problem and finishes with "pero, diay, pura vida", they are not telling you everything is fine. They are telling you they are not going to let it ruin their day.

That is what does not fit in a dictionary, and it is exactly what you learn by talking to a Costa Rican rather than to an app.
